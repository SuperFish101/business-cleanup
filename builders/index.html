Straight answers

The questions people actually ask.

I've been burned by a consultant before. Why is this different?

Most consultants hand you a binder, or build you something on a platform only they know how to run. When they leave, so does the value. You've probably got one of those binders on a shelf right now.

You get plain files you own. No app, no subscription, no vendor. If you never speak to me again, everything keeps working.

And it's written in plain English, not code. Any person can open it, read it, and know exactly how to do the job. You can point AI at it if you want to, and that's where a lot of the time savings come from. But you don't have to. That's the whole design.

Is this just AI thrown at my business?

No, and I'd be suspicious of anyone selling it that way. The split is 60% infrastructure, 30% orchestration, 10% AI. In plain terms:

60% is organizing your operation so it's clean and understandable to anyone who looks at it. 30% is walking your actual build process and fixing the places work gets handed off and dropped — sales to build, build to trades, trades to inspection. 10% is taking the repetitive stuff off your plate so you're back on the jobs that need you.

AI on a disorganized business just makes a mess faster. The organizing is where almost all the value is. Automation goes on last, and only where it genuinely saves you time.

How much of my time does this take?

Honest answer: it depends on the scope. Most of your time lands early, while I'm learning how you actually build. After that it's short check-ins.

I'll give you a real number once I've seen the job, and I'd rather do that than put a guess on a webpage. What I can tell you is that the building is my job. If this needed twenty hours of your time, it wouldn't be solving your problem.

What if my team won't use it?

Your subs aren't logging into anything. Your site guys aren't learning software. I'm not adding a tool on top of anyone's day.

What changes is that your office stops chasing and your trades stop guessing, because the schedule, the specs and the updates come from one place instead of six. For most crews this is less work, not more. That's usually why it sticks.

What does it cost?

Most engagements run four figures, flat. Fixed scope, one number, no hourly billing and no monthly fee.

I don't quote before I've seen your business and scoped the job, because a two-house-a-year custom builder and a twenty-lot developer are very different work. That's what the 20-minute call is for.

What happens after the 30 days?

It's yours. You keep the system whether or not we ever work together again.

You also get the 90-day tune-up — I come back and fix whatever drifted — and a direct line to me for quick questions. Neither costs extra.

I only build a few houses a year. Is this overkill?

No. Smaller builders are often the clearest fit, because everything genuinely does live in one head, and that head is out on site all day.

The problem isn't how many houses you put up. It's having more to do than time to do it, and things slipping between the jobs as a result.

Have you ever built anything?

Straight answer: I've never built a house, and I'm not going to pretend I have.

What I'm also not is a computer guy who's never been on a site. My dad was a land surveyor and I worked construction alongside him growing up. I've built custom kitchen cabinets, poured concrete countertops, made thirty-odd pieces of furniture, and grew up on a farm. I know what the work is, and I know what a job sounds like when it's going sideways.

What I've actually done for a living is be second in command at three companies — untangling operations, building the systems, working out why things kept falling over and fixing it. That's the job here. You build the houses. I fix the business around them.
